Only when there are no danger signs Five things to check before calling for a repair. A thermostat setting, dirty filter, or blocked vent can interrupt heating. These basic observations may help explain the problem; they are not a substitute for diagnosis. If you smell gas, suspect CO, see smoke, or hear new banging or grinding, stop and seek help. Check accessible exterior vents From ground level, look for loose snow blocking intake or exhaust openings. Clear only loose snow you can safely reach. Do not climb, chip ice, disconnect pipes, or alter the vent termination; call if ice or a blockage remains. Inspect the air filter Turn the system off according to its instructions before changing the filter. Replace a clogged filter with the specified size and type, and reinstall the cover. Do not run the furnace without its filter or open combustion compartments. Look at the external power switch and breaker Check whether the furnace’s external switch was accidentally turned off or the breaker has tripped. Confirm thermostat settings Set the thermostat to HEAT and above room temperature. Replace batteries if your model uses them. If it locks out again, stop and call. Common cold-weather furnace failures. Similar symptoms can have different causes. Here is what a technician may investigate. Ignitor or flame-sensor trouble The inducer starts, but the burners do not light—or they light briefly and shut off. An ignitor, flame sensor, gas-supply issue, or control fault may be involved. Testing identifies the cause before parts are replaced. Condensate or pressure-switch faults A blocked drain, restricted vent, wind-related venting issue, or pressure-switch circuit fault can interrupt operation. A pressure-switch code needs diagnosis; it does not automatically mean the switch itself has failed. Inducer or blower problems Humming without normal airflow, grinding, or weak circulation can point to a motor, capacitor where fitted, or another mechanical or electrical issue. Stop using equipment that makes new grinding or scraping sounds and arrange an inspection. High-limit or rollout safety shutdowns Restricted airflow or an unsafe combustion condition can trigger a protective shutdown. A running blower does not identify which safety control is involved. Never bypass these switches or attempt to reset a rollout switch yourself. Rural heating On propane outside Great Falls? Check your tank gauge.
